PPC Audit Report: What to Include, With a Copy-Ready Template (2026)

A PPC audit is only as useful as the report that comes out of it. Many audits end as a long spreadsheet of findings that nobody acts on, because the person who has to approve the changes cannot tell which three things matter most. A good PPC audit report fixes that: it explains what is wrong, how much it is likely costing, and what to do first.

This guide covers what a PPC audit report should contain, how to rank the fixes, a template you can copy, and the mistakes that make reports get ignored. It is written for small business owners, freelancers and in-house marketers who audit Google Ads or Meta Ads accounts and need someone else to say yes to the changes.

What a PPC audit report is (and is not)

The audit is the work: checking account structure, tracking, keywords, search terms, bidding, ads, landing pages and budgets. The report is the document that turns that work into decisions. It is not a dump of every setting you looked at. If you want the full list of checks, use our PPC audit resource or the step-by-step guide to a PPC account audit. The report is what you hand over afterwards.

A reader should be able to answer three questions within two minutes of opening it:

  • Is the account healthy, or is money being wasted?
  • What are the most important problems, in order?
  • What exactly will change, who will do it, and by when?

The 7 sections every PPC audit report needs

1. Executive summary

Half a page at most. State the date range you reviewed, total spend in that period, the main conversion goal, and your overall verdict in one sentence. Then list the top three findings and the top three recommended actions. Many readers stop here, so it has to stand on its own.

2. Scope and data sources

List the accounts and campaigns reviewed, the date range, and where the numbers came from (for example Google Ads, Meta Ads Manager, Google Analytics 4, a CRM). Note anything you could not check, such as offline conversions or call tracking. This protects you later if someone asks why a number differs from another report.

3. Conversion tracking check

Put tracking first among the findings, because every other conclusion depends on it. Record which conversion actions exist, which are set as primary, whether any are counted twice, and whether the numbers roughly match what the business sees in its own sales or lead records. If tracking is broken, say so plainly: optimizing bids on bad data makes results worse, not better.

4. Findings by area

Group findings into a few clear areas so the reader is not scrolling through a random list:

  • Structure: campaign and ad group organization, overlapping campaigns, naming.
  • Targeting: locations, schedules, devices, audiences, and network settings such as Search Partners or Display expansion.
  • Keywords and search terms: irrelevant search terms, missing negative keywords, match types.
  • Bidding and budget: bid strategy versus the amount of conversion data, campaigns limited by budget, spend going to low performers.
  • Ads and assets: ad strength, missing sitelinks or callouts, ads that do not match the keyword intent.
  • Landing pages: page speed, mobile layout, message match between ad and page, form friction.

For each finding, write one line for what you saw, one line for why it matters, and the evidence (a screenshot, a filtered report, or the exact numbers).

5. Estimated impact

Where you can, attach a number from the account itself rather than a guess. For example: spend in the last 30 days on search terms with no conversions that are clearly irrelevant to the business, or spend on locations outside the service area. Label estimates as estimates. A reader trusts a report that shows its working far more than one that promises a percentage improvement.

6. Prioritized action plan

This is the heart of the report. Turn the findings into a numbered list of actions, each with an owner, an effort level and a target date. Rank them with a simple rule, explained in the next section.

7. Measurement plan

Say how you will know the changes worked: which metrics you will compare (cost per conversion, conversion rate, wasted spend), over which period, and when the follow-up review will happen. Two to four weeks after the main changes is a common checkpoint, since bid strategies need time to settle.

How to prioritize fixes: impact versus effort

The easiest way to rank recommendations is a two-by-two of impact and effort:

  • High impact, low effort (do first): fixing a double-counted conversion, adding obvious negative keywords, switching location targeting from "presence or interest" to "presence" for a local business, pausing ads that point to a broken page.
  • High impact, high effort (plan next): restructuring campaigns, rebuilding landing pages, setting up offline conversion imports.
  • Low impact, low effort (batch together): naming clean-up, adding missing callouts.
  • Low impact, high effort (park): anything that is nice to have but does not change spend or results.

Tracking problems always go to the top regardless of effort, because they affect every other decision.

Common mistakes that make audit reports get ignored

  • Too long. Forty pages of screenshots hide the five things that matter. Put detail in an appendix.
  • No order. A flat list of 60 issues gives the reader no idea where to start.
  • Jargon without meaning. "Impression share lost to rank is 40%" means little to an owner. Add the plain-English consequence.
  • No evidence. Every finding should point to a report, filter or screenshot someone can check.
  • Promises instead of estimates. Avoid guaranteeing results. Show the spend affected and let the follow-up review prove the gain.
  • No follow-up date. Without a review date, the report becomes a one-off document instead of the start of an improvement cycle.

FAQ

What should a PPC audit report include?

An executive summary, the scope and data sources, a conversion tracking check, findings grouped by area with evidence, estimated impact, a prioritized action plan with owners and dates, and a measurement plan with a follow-up review date.

How long should a PPC audit report be?

Long enough to support the decisions and no longer. For most small business accounts, a one-page summary plus a few pages of findings and an action table is enough. Put supporting screenshots in an appendix.

How often should I audit a PPC account?

A full audit once or twice a year is common, with lighter monthly reviews of search terms, budgets and conversion tracking. Run a full audit sooner after major changes, such as a new website or a new conversion goal.

What is the first thing to check in a PPC audit?

Conversion tracking. If conversions are missing, duplicated or counting the wrong action, every other metric and every bid strategy decision in the account is affected.

Can I use the same report template for Google Ads and Meta Ads?

Yes. The seven sections work for both. Only the findings change: for Meta Ads you would review areas such as audience overlap, creative fatigue and pixel or Conversions API setup instead of keywords and search terms.

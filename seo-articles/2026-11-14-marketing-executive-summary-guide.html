How to Write a Marketing Executive Summary (With a Copy-Ready Template)

An executive summary is the part of a marketing report most people actually read. Owners, managers and clients rarely have time to scroll through campaign tables; they want to know in under a minute what happened, why it happened and what you plan to do next. This guide shows how to write a one-page summary for Google Ads and Meta Ads reporting, gives you a template you can copy, and lists the mistakes that make summaries useless.

What a marketing executive summary is (and is not)

A marketing executive summary is a short section, usually half a page to one page, at the top of a monthly or quarterly report. It states the most important results against the goals that were agreed, explains the main reasons behind them, and recommends specific next steps.

It is not a list of every metric, a copy of the platform dashboard, or a place to hide bad news. If the reader stopped after the summary, they should still be able to make a decision.

The five parts every summary needs

1. The goal and the period

Start with one sentence that sets the frame: which channels, which dates, and which goal the work is measured against. For example: "Google Ads and Meta Ads, October 1-31, measured against a target of 120 qualified leads at or below $45 per lead." Without the goal, numbers have no meaning.

2. Headline results

Give three to five numbers that map directly to the goal, each with a comparison: previous period, same period last year, or target. Typical choices are conversions, cost per conversion, return on ad spend, revenue and total spend. Keep each one to a single line.

3. What drove the results

Explain the two or three biggest causes in plain language. Good drivers are specific and checkable: "The new search campaign for emergency repairs produced 38% of leads at a lower cost than the account average" is useful; "optimizations improved performance" is not.

4. Problems and risks

Name anything that went wrong or could go wrong: tracking gaps, a landing page outage, rising costs in one campaign, a creative that is wearing out. Readers trust a summary more when it is honest about weak spots.

5. Recommendations and decisions needed

End with numbered next steps, each with an owner and a date where possible. If you need a decision from the reader, such as a budget change or approval for new creative, say so clearly in the last line.

A copy-ready template

Paste this into your report and replace the brackets.

Period and goal: [Channels], [dates]. Goal: [target metric and value].

Results:

  • [Metric 1]: [value] ([+/- x%] vs [comparison])
  • [Metric 2]: [value] ([+/- x%] vs [comparison])
  • [Metric 3]: [value] ([+/- x%] vs [comparison])
  • Total spend: [value] ([x%] of budget)

What drove it: [Driver 1, one sentence]. [Driver 2, one sentence].

Issues: [Problem or risk, and what is being done about it].

Next steps: 1) [Action, owner, date]. 2) [Action, owner, date]. 3) [Action, owner, date].

Decision needed: [Yes/No question for the reader, or "None this month"].

Choosing the right metrics for each channel

Pick metrics the reader cares about, not the ones that look best this month.

  • Google Ads search campaigns: conversions, cost per conversion, conversion rate and, for e-commerce, conversion value and return on ad spend. Impression share is worth adding when budget limits are holding results back.
  • Meta Ads: results for the campaign objective (purchases, leads or app installs), cost per result, and frequency for audiences that see the same ads many times.
  • Across channels: blended cost per lead or customer, total revenue influenced, and spend against budget.

Click-through rate, impressions and reach belong in the detailed report. Put them in the summary only when they explain a result.

Common mistakes that make summaries useless

  • No comparison. "92 leads" means nothing unless the reader knows the target or last month's figure.
  • Too many numbers. More than five headline metrics turns the summary into a dashboard.
  • Platform jargon. Terms like "learning phase" or "Smart Bidding" need a short explanation for non-specialists.
  • Mixing attribution models without saying so. Google Ads and Meta Ads count conversions differently, so their totals often add up to more than the sales you actually made. Say which source each number comes from.
  • Vague next steps. "Continue optimizing" is not a plan. Name the campaign, the change and the date.
  • Burying bad news. If a goal was missed, say it in the first lines along with the reason and the fix.

A short worked example

Here is how a summary for a local home-services business might read. The figures are illustrative only.

Period and goal: Google Ads and Meta Ads, October 1-31. Goal: 120 qualified leads at or below $45 each.

Results: 131 qualified leads (+9% vs target); cost per lead $41.20 (-8% vs September); total spend $5,397 (98% of budget).

What drove it: The new search campaign for same-day repairs brought in 50 leads at $33 each. Meta lead ads in two ZIP codes added 22 leads after the creative was refreshed on October 9.

Issues: Call tracking failed for three days (October 14-16), so phone leads in that window are undercounted. Fixed on October 17.

Next steps: 1) Move $600 from the general brand campaign to same-day repairs by November 3. 2) Test a second Meta creative by November 10. 3) Add a weekly call-tracking check.

Decision needed: Approve a 10% budget increase for November? Yes/No.

How to write summaries faster

Most of the time spent on a summary goes into pulling numbers and working out what changed. A few habits help:

  • Agree the goal and the three to five headline metrics with the reader once, and keep them the same every month.
  • Keep a running log of changes you make during the month (new campaigns, budget moves, creative swaps). The "what drove it" section almost writes itself from that log.
  • Draft the summary last, after you have looked at the detailed data, but place it first in the report.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a marketing executive summary be?

Half a page to one page. If it runs longer, move detail into the main report and keep only the results, drivers, issues and next steps.

Should the summary come first or last in the report?

First. Write it last, once you understand the data, but place it at the top so readers see it before anything else.

What if the campaign missed its goal?

Say so in the results line, explain the main reason in one or two sentences, and give a specific plan to fix it. Hiding a miss damages trust more than the miss itself.

Can AI write the executive summary for me?

AI can produce a useful first draft from your numbers and notes, but you should check every figure against the ad platforms and rewrite the reasoning in your own words. The reader is relying on your judgment, not on the tool's.

How often should I send an executive summary?

Monthly works for most small businesses. Weekly summaries make sense during launches or when spend is high; quarterly summaries suit owners who only need the big picture.
